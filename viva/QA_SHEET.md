# Viva questions and honest answers

Answer 9 holds the evaluation numbers as of 18 Nov 2026 (14 of 15 prompts completed). If the missing counter prompt is run later (`python3 tools/evaluate_live.py --only s1`), refresh the figures from `backend/evaluation/summary.md`.

**1. Why should anyone trust a PASS?**
The simulator, not the model, decides. A PASS needs a whole-line `Passed: N` / `Failed: 0` summary with N above zero, no `FAIL` or `ERROR` line contradicting it, and a clean simulator exit. The design under test is not allowed to print the summary or end the run, so it cannot write its own verdict. Zero executed checks is never a PASS. It is still *evidence*, not a proof.

**2. Who checks the checker?** (the strongest question to expect)
After a PASS the app injects up to 12 small bugs into the design (`+` to `-`, `&` to `|`, `==` to `!=`, a flipped constant or case label, a negated `if`, ...) and reruns the same testbench on each one. The card shows "caught N of M". On the five built-in designs the testbenches catch every injected bug except two. One is provably equivalent: replacing the XOR in a full adder's carry term by OR still gives the majority function. The other is a genuine small blind spot, not an equivalent mutant: swapping the counter's clock edge (`posedge` to `negedge`) goes unnoticed because the testbench's sampling cannot tell the two apart. I report that openly. A deliberately weak testbench scores below half and triggers a warning. *Limit:* a survivor may be an equivalent mutant, so the score is evidence about the testbench, not a proof.

**3. Does one model write both the design and the testbench?**
Yes, in the live demo. The testbench is generated from the specification only, never from the RTL, and the repair loop is forbidden from weakening it. A different model for the testbench is a supported setting, and I tried it: on a counter whose correctness I knew, the free models either timed out, produced a testbench that never connected the design (it was reported as FAIL, not a false PASS) or did not compile. So independence is supported but not reliable on the free tier.

**4. What stops the repair loop from cheating by deleting checks?**
A minimum count of real check sites in the testbench, a check that it still instantiates the design, validation of the model's replacement design and testbench, and, after a repair, a re-run of the original testbench on the final design with an explicit warning if it still fails.

**5. Is this safe to run on untrusted input?**
It is defence in depth on a developer machine, not a jail. System tasks are an allow-list (file and process access is rejected before compile and before lint), the environment is scrubbed so API keys never reach the simulator, CPU, file-size and memory limits apply (the memory limit is enforced on Linux, not macOS), and at most two simulations run at once. For multi-user use the simulator belongs in a container.

**6. Does it give area, timing or power?**
Timing and area, yes, as a pre-layout estimate; power, no. After a PASS, Yosys maps the design to the open SkyWater 130 nm cell library and OpenSTA (the timing engine used in OpenROAD) reports the worst setup slack at the target clock, the maximum frequency and the area in µm². For example the 4-bit counter is 11 cells, 150 µm², and meets 100 MHz with 9.27 ns slack; the 8-bit ALU misses a 2000 MHz target (maximum about 371 MHz). A design with no clock, such as the ALU, is reported as its longest input-to-output path against the time budget instead of "meets a clock". The limits: one typical corner, no placement or routing so no wire delay, an ideal clock, zero I/O delays, setup analysis only. It answers "is this logic near the target?", not sign-off.

**7. Why Icarus Verilog? What about UVM?**
It is free and scriptable. Icarus 12.0 accepts most synthesizable SystemVerilog (`logic`, `always_ff`, `return`, `enum`) and rejects a specific set (`break`/`continue`, `disable` of a function, `inside`, streaming operators, concurrent assertions, class constraints). UVM needs the UVM class library, which Icarus does not implement, so UVM is export-only and never simulated. Be precise if asked: only ALU-like designs get a real reference model in the exported scoreboard; for other designs the scoreboard is a placeholder with a hook for your own model.

**8. Is the coverage real coverage?**
Toggle coverage is real: it is measured from the simulator's own waveform file, for design signals only (each bit must be seen going 0 to 1 and 1 to 0). On the deliberately buggy counter it reports "count[0] never changed", which is the bug itself. Line and branch coverage are not measured (that needs an instrumenting simulator such as Verilator); any other coverage number shown is what the testbench prints about itself and is labelled as such.

**9. What did your evaluation show?**
Fifteen prompts (7 combinational, 6 sequential, 2 FSM) were run live with the free DeepSeek model, with no offline fallback; 14 completed. Of those 14, 8 passed on the first attempt, 6 passed after one self-repair and 0 did not pass; the median was 360 s per run (3 to 13 minutes). The fifteenth, the 4-bit counter, did not complete: its testbench generation timed out on three separate attempts (360 s, 600 s, 600 s) while the other prompts finished, so it is reported as "no result", never as a pass or a failure (a counter run earlier in development did pass with the same model). The strength score caught 102 of 111 injected bugs (92%); 11 designs caught all of theirs, and the three that missed some were c1 (10 of 12), c7 (9 of 12) and f2 (7 of 11). Because that score is about the testbench and not about the verdict, I also checked every one of the 14 passing designs against an independent, hand-written reference testbench (not written by the model): all 14 are correct, so there was no false PASS. Those references were measured too, by injecting every possible one-token bug; every bug they missed is an equivalent change, unreachable code, or behaviour the prompt does not define. The records, designs, testbenches and references are in `backend/evaluation/`.

**10. How is this different from AutoBench, CorrectBench or VerilogEval?**
AutoBench and CorrectBench generate (and in CorrectBench self-correct) testbenches for HDL designs; VerilogEval is a benchmark of design generation against golden references. SiliconScribe is an end-to-end tool on a single open-source simulator that generates the testbench independently from the specification, forbids the repair loop from weakening it, reports every executed check, and measures the testbench's strength by mutation. It does not reproduce their evaluation protocols, so I make no performance comparison.

**11. What happens when the model or the network fails?**
The app says so. Before any live call has succeeded it may fall back to a scripted demo, but that is labelled "Demo fallback". After a live success, a later outage fails loudly instead of swapping in an unrelated design.

**12. Why do live runs take minutes?**
Free-tier models are slow: generating the testbench alone has taken 2-3 minutes, and one evaluation run took 11 minutes. The UI shows seconds since the last update so slow looks different from stuck. A paid or local model would be much faster.

**13. Is the interface accessible?**
An automated axe-core audit of every tab, for a passing and a failing run, reports zero violations (structure, names, heading order and colour contrast), and the structural rules run in the test suite. That is an automated audit, not a user study.

**14. What are the main limitations?**
One model for both stages; free-tier speed and quota; the strength score is a sample of 12 syntactic mutants; timing is pre-layout on one corner and there is no power analysis; only toggle coverage is measured; the specification is still written by a model unless you review it, and there are no assertions (the formal check covers only netlist = RTL); sandbox is not a jail; evaluated on small designs, not full SoC blocks.

**15. What would you do next?**
Power from the simulation's own activity (OpenSTA can read the VCD), mutation-guided testbench hardening (missed bugs go back to the model), coverage-driven tests, place-and-route with OpenROAD for post-layout timing, SVA assertions with formal property checks, a multi-model comparison on the 15 prompts, and export to a Tiny Tapeout shuttle for real silicon.

**16. How do you know the timing and area numbers are right?**
They are checked three independent ways, and the check is a script you can run (`python3 tools/verify_mapping.py`, about 30 seconds). (1) The mapped gate netlist is simulated against the same testbench as the RTL, using cell models Yosys builds from the liberty file, and must give identical pass counts: it does on all 19 designs, including the sequential ones. On top of that, 16 of 18 designs are now formally proven equal to their RTL (see Q20). (2) Area and cell count are recomputed from the written netlist and the cell library and must equal what Yosys reported. (3) The minimum clock period implied by the slack must not change when the target clock changes. I also tried to break it on purpose and fixed what I found: a design built from sub-modules reported only the top module's area (about 40% too low) and missed its clock; clocks not named `clk` were not recognised; a tri-state was silently turned into a wire and reported as "meets 100 MHz"; a combinational loop gave a nonsense maximum frequency; and a testbench that overrides a parameter would have timed a different circuit from the one simulated. Now it times the whole flattened design, reads the clock from the netlist, and refuses (with a reason) tri-states, latches, loops and derived clocks instead of printing a number. The limits stay: one corner, no wires, setup only.

**17. Can you change the AI model, and what if the free one stalls?**
Yes, per run, from the Model menu. It lists every model the OpenCode app offers (free ones first; the rest behind "Show other models", because they may use credits), and two more tabs use Cloudflare Workers AI and NVIDIA NIM when their keys are set in the environment (a git-ignored file, never the web page). The choice is sent with the run and remembered, and the result shows which model wrote the design. A model you pick on purpose is never silently replaced by the scripted demo: if it fails you get a plain message and pick another. Model names are validated before they reach the OpenCode command, and the page never accepts or stores a Cloudflare token. In my evaluation the 4-bit counter prompt stalled three times on the default model; the same prompt passed on another free model (`hy4-flash-free`, 173 of 173 checks), which is exactly the case the menu is for. Cloudflare was tested live: the catalogue (40 text models) loads and a chat answers in about 2 seconds. A full run on its `gpt-oss-120b` wrote a correct counter but a testbench whose watchdog timer was shorter than its own test, so no summary was printed and the app refused to call it PASS (ERROR, 0 checks) instead of passing it: model choice matters, and the verdict rules hold whichever model is used. Limit: Cloudflare's free allowance is 10,000 "neurons" a day, so it is a backup, not the main path. NVIDIA NIM was also tested live: the catalogue loads (15 chat models) and a chat answers in under a second on the fastest, but some of its free-queue models took 1 to 5 minutes for one reply, and a model the app was configured for earlier (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on NIM) had been retired two days before, which is why the lists are read live and never hard-coded.

**18. What if the model misreads the prompt? Doesn't the testbench just agree with the design?**
That is the main weakness of any model-written verification, and the app has three controls for it. (1) The specification can be shown before anything is built: tick "Review the specification before generating", read the ports, behaviour and constraints, fix them, and the run uses exactly that text. (2) A different model, even from a different provider, can write the testbench ("Testbench written by"). Then a failing check is reported, never rewritten away by the design model; only a testbench that does not compile or hangs may be repaired. If the two models disagree the result says so. (3) If a PASS only came after a repair rewrote the testbench, the app asks for a fresh testbench from the same specification and runs the final design against it; a disagreement puts a warning on the PASS. A real example: with one free OpenCode model writing the counter and an NVIDIA model writing the testbench, the run ended FAIL (124 of 146 checks): the counter was correct and the testbench sampled one cycle early, so the app refused to pass it and said the two models disagree. What it does not do is decide automatically which side is wrong; a person compares the first failing check with the specification. It also does not replace assertions or formal proof.

**19. Can I see the actual gates the AI's design turns into?**
Yes: the Schematic tab has three views. Block diagram shows the ports. RTL cells shows what Yosys builds before mapping: adders, multiplexers, comparators and flip-flops. Gate level shows every single-bit gate and flip-flop after synthesis (Yosys' generic gates, not the sky130 cells, which keeps the drawing readable). They are drawn from the netlist of that exact run with the open-source netlistsvg; designs above 400 cells are not drawn because the picture stops being readable.

**20. You say the netlist is "formally proven". What exactly is proven?**
That the sky130 gate netlist the timing numbers describe computes exactly the same function as the RTL. For combinational designs Yosys builds a miter (both circuits side by side, outputs compared) and its SAT solver shows no input value makes them differ: that covers every input, not a sample. For sequential designs the registers are paired by name and temporal induction proves each pair stays equal. It does NOT prove the RTL is correct: that is the testbench's and the reference checks' job. Result: 16 of 18 designs proven; a netlist I sabotaged on purpose (one NAND changed to a NOR) is reported as different.

**21. Why are the two FSMs only "not proven"?**
Synthesis re-encodes FSM states (for example to one-hot), so the registers of the netlist no longer pair up with the RTL's by name, and induction cannot decide. The honest answer is "not proven", which the app shows; it never turns an undecided proof into a pass. The gate-level simulation check still passes for both. A proof that matches re-encoded states would need a state-mapping step or keeping the original encoding.

**22. What does the critical path table tell you?**
The slowest path pin by pin, with each sky130 cell's delay: where the time goes. For the 8-bit ALU it is the ripple-carry chain: six maj3 (majority = carry) cells at about 0.36 to 0.39 ns each, 2.69 ns in total. That is exactly the case a carry-lookahead or carry-select adder would shorten, so the table shows what to change to make the design faster.

**23. How did you check your own tool before the viva?**
Nine reviewers, each acting as one engineering role (ASIC, verification, backend, security, LLM integration, frontend, UX, test, release), reviewed everything except the documents and reported about 110 findings. Each was reproduced with a real run, fixed, and pinned by a test that fails when the fix is undone (I undid every fix once to prove it). An independent reviewer then got only the findings and the change set and tried to break the fixes: it found a macro-argument sandbox bypass, a regex slowdown the fixes introduced, and three regressions, all fixed the same way. The 123 saved runs give identical verdicts with the old and new code.

**24. What does "PASS, disputed" mean?**
The simulator said PASS, but the run's own evidence contradicts it: the testbench was changed during repair and the ORIGINAL testbench, or a fresh second-opinion testbench, fails the final design. The app does not guess which side is right; it refuses to show a plain green PASS. The live FIFO run is the example: 4134/4134 on the rewritten testbench, 482 failures on the original one, so it is shown as disputed.

**25. What was the most serious thing the review found?**
A testbench could write a file outside its folder: first with a comment between `$dumpfile` and its argument, later (found by the independent reviewer) by passing the `$` of `$fopen` through a macro argument. The rules now also run on comment-stripped text and on the macro-expanded source from Icarus' own preprocessor. On the honesty side: a repair could keep the bug and edit the expected values, and the screen still said PASS; now only a testbench that did not compile may be rewritten freely, and a contradicted PASS is shown as disputed.


**26. Why can a simulation hang although the design is correct? (the SR latch run, 20 Nov)**
A live "design sr latch" run passed 552 checks and then timed out. The testbench released the forbidden s_n=r_n=0 input on both pins in the same time step. With zero gate delay both NAND gates switch together, 1,1 to 0,0 to 1,1, forever, as delta cycles at one simulated time, so time stops and a `#20000` watchdog can never fire; only the 30 s wall-clock limit ends it. Real silicon settles because one gate is always slightly faster. TIMEOUT was the right verdict, but the screen said "0 checks, 0%". Now the simulator stops `vvp` with SIGTERM first (it flushes its output; SIGKILL lost the tail), shows "552 checks before stop" with no pass rate, explains the likely zero-delay loop to the user and the repair model, and the testbench prompt says to leave a forbidden state one input at a time. For the demo, avoid gate-level latches and ring oscillators.


## Ask about this run (debugging agent, added 24 Nov 2026, after the report was submitted)

**27. What is "Ask about this run"?**
A chat in the sidebar. You ask about the code in the editor; the server simulates that code again in the same sandbox, and the model then answers by calling tools on that real simulation: the failing checks, a signal's value at a time and just before it, the changes in a window, a cycle-by-cycle table at each clock edge, when a signal held a value, which lines assign a signal, numbered source lines, and testing a fix (at most 3 per question). It must query the simulation at least once before it may answer. Times in the answer are links that move the waveform cursor.

**28. Why should I trust its answer?**
Trust the fix, not the prose. Every fix in an answer is simulated again by the server and the card shows that verdict ("PASS, 57 of 57 checks"), never the model's claim. The explanation is model text. On the SR latch that timed out, the fix passed 558 of 558, but the explanation blamed the hold state instead of the testbench releasing both inputs from 0,0 at once. That is why the explanation is labelled as an AI answer and the fix carries its own verdict.

**29. Can it tell when the testbench, not the design, is wrong?**
Yes, in the run I made. With a correct counter and a testbench that expects 3 where 2 is right, it answered "Suspect: Testbench": the check runs at the negedge at 560 ns, before the third rising edge. Its testbench fix passed 57 of 57, and the app warns that a testbench fix changes the checker itself and can make a wrong design pass. That took 219 s.

**30. How is this different from your repair loop?**
The repair loop is a fixed pipeline: the orchestrator decides what runs and the model only rewrites code from the log. The agent chooses what to inspect (which signal, which time window, which lines) and answers follow-up questions, but every tool result comes from the simulator or the source, and every fix is re-simulated. "Only the simulator decides" still holds.

**31. Why plain JSON instead of the provider's function calling?**
The app uses four model sources, including the OpenCode app's command line, which has no function-calling interface. One JSON object per reply works with all of them. Replies that are not valid JSON are sent back once; a second slip is shown as unchecked text.

**32. What happens with no model or a failed model?**
It never swaps in a fake answer. With no model connected, a model error (quota, timeout) or ten steps without an answer, it shows "Rule-based evidence (no AI)": the first failing check, the port values at that time, the clock cycles before it, and the lines that assign the outputs, with a note that it is not an AI diagnosis.

**33. Limits of the agent?**
Explanations are not verified; answers take 30 s to 5 minutes on free models; the "drivers" tool is a text search, not an elaborated netlist; Icarus only; one run per case on small designs, so it is a demonstration, not a benchmark. It has 41 backend and 10 frontend tests of its own (632 and 277 in total), including sabotage checks that the tests fail when the fix check or the edge sampling is broken.


## Questions from the morning of the viva (25 Nov 2026)

**34. Why do some runs fail at the testbench step, and what does the tool do about it?**
Writing the RTL is the easy part for a language model; writing a correct, independent testbench is the hard part. It is 3 to 10 times longer than the RTL, every expected value has to be worked out from the specification without seeing the RTL, and the timing of each check relative to the clock must be right. I measured it: 69 models were in the menu; 29 failed a one-message check and are now hidden; of the 40 that answered, 18 wrote both a correct counter and a correct 8-bit ALU (judged by Icarus with reference testbenches), but only one, DeepSeek, wrote a testbench on the first try that passed the correct counter (173 of 173) and caught both planted bugs. Others timed out, were cut off, did not compile, or failed the correct design. The tool never hides this: a model that fails is named with the reason, and since 25 Nov the run asks DeepSeek to write the testbench instead and says so in the timeline and in the result. A testbench that is valid but wrong is a different case: the simulator then reports FAIL and the repair loop and the second opinion deal with it.

**35. Is it scalable?**
Today it is a local single-user tool, and I say so in the report: one machine, at most two simulations at once, a 20 requests per minute limit, a local SQLite file. The architecture scales because every run is independent (its own workspace, simulation and result) and the simulator part is cheap: the pipeline without the model takes about 50 to 150 ms per design. The bottleneck is the language model, minutes per run on free models. Scaling means a job queue with worker machines, a container per simulation (my sandbox is defence in depth, not a jail), PostgreSQL and accounts, and a paid model API. Bigger designs need a hierarchical flow: the specification already fixes the interface, so sub-modules can be generated and verified separately and then integrated. I have not tested any of that, so I do not claim it.

**36. How does this compare with what Synopsys and Cadence offer?**
It does not compete with them. They have industrial simulators, formal tools, place and route and sign-off timing built over decades, with foundry-certified libraries, and their AI products sit on top of those engines (flow tuning for power, performance and area, faster coverage closure, AI-assisted debug, generative assistants). SiliconScribe is the same kind of AI loop on open-source engines at small scale: prompt to RTL plus an independent testbench, a debugger that reads the waveform, testbench strength by injected bugs, and pre-layout timing on sky130. My contribution is the trust loop: the simulator decides, and every result says how much it is worth. The gaps are accuracy (pre-layout, one corner), scale (small blocks), validation (15 prompts) and tools (no commercial simulator, so no full UVM or concurrent assertions).

**37. If the testbench never sees the RTL, how does it know the signal names?**
From the specification. Before any code is written the prompt becomes a specification with the module name and every port (name, direction, width) and the behaviour. The RTL generator must use exactly those ports; the testbench generator gets the same ports and the behaviour, but not the RTL. A testbench treats the design as a black box: it drives the inputs and checks the outputs, so it never needs the RTL's internal names. Limits: it cannot check internal signals directly, and the repair loop does see both files, which is why a testbench changed by a repair is flagged, the original is re-run, and a fresh second-opinion testbench is written from the specification again.
